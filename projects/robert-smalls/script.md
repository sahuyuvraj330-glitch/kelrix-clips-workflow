## Task 2: Research — verified timeline

- Date: May 13, 1862, early morning — NPS; HistoryNet
- Ship: Planter, 147-foot sidewheel steamer built in Charleston in 1860, leased to the Confederate Army, used as dispatch and transport boat and for harbor defenses — NPS
- Crew: 3 white officers and 7 enslaved crewmen; the white officers often slept ashore despite standing orders — NPS
- Smalls: Born April 5, 1839, in Beaufort, SC; age 23 in 1862 — NPS; HistoryNet
- Hat: Smalls took the captain's straw hat and jacket and imitated his posture — HistoryNet (Westwood); NPS
- Departure: About 3:00 a.m. the boiler fired up. The wharf guard recalled it moving off around 3:30 — NPS; HistoryNet
- Families: The crew's wives and children waited on another boat, the Etiwan, and were picked up en route — NPS
- Fort Sumter: About 4:15 a.m. Smalls gave the proper signal and the sentry let them pass — NPS; HistoryNet
- Surrender: They raised a bedsheet. The USS Onward had readied its guns, and its acting captain, John Frederick Nickels, thought the Planter was a threat — NPS; HistoryNet
- Cargo: Four extra artillery pieces were aboard, plus the ship's own two. HistoryNet describes the cargo as armament meant for Fort Ripley — NPS; HistoryNet
- Reward: Congress passed a bill and Lincoln signed it May 30, 1862, giving the crew half the appraised value. Smalls got $1,500 — HistoryNet; SC General Assembly resolution
- Union service: Piloted for the Union; the SC resolution says he returned to the Planter in October 1862 — HistoryNet; SC General Assembly resolution
- Later career: Elected to the U.S. House in 1874; NPS says five terms between 1875 and 1887 — NPS

### Established, disputed, or uncertain

- Established: the date, the ship, the departure before dawn, the disguise, passing Fort Sumter on the signal, the bedsheet, and the congressional reward.
- Number of people aboard: NPS and HistoryNet say 16. A South Carolina House resolution says 17. Claude used “sixteen,” the NPS figure.
- Who was at the wheel: NPS says Abram Allston, an experienced pilot, manned the wheel while Smalls wore the captain's coat and hat. Other accounts call Smalls the wheelman. The script avoids taking a side.
- Crew names and ranks: NPS lists the white officers as Relyea, Hancock and Pitcher. The SC resolution names Relyea, Samuel H. Smith and Zerich Pitcher. The script doesn't name them.
- Quote Claude did not use: HistoryNet (Westwood, 1986) gives Smalls's greeting to the Onward as “Good morning, Sir! I've brought you some of the old United States guns, sir!” — couldn't trace it to a primary document, so left out.
- Du Pont's praise: HistoryNet quotes Rear Admiral Du Pont as calling it “one of the coolest and most gallant naval acts of war.” Not verified from the original report, so out of the narration.
- Prize amount: the $9,000 appraisal was widely criticized as low. A House committee in 1883 called it “absurdly low,” and Congress passed an additional payment in 1900. Good long-form point, doesn't fit 90 seconds.
- Perspective: this was a plan by the whole enslaved crew and their families. The script uses “Smalls and the other enslaved crew” instead of a lone-hero framing.

### Voiceover (198 words, about 75–90 seconds)

Before dawn on May 13, 1862, a Confederate steamer called the Planter slipped away from a wharf in Charleston, South Carolina. At the rail stood a man in the captain's straw hat. He was not the captain. He was Robert Smalls, a twenty-three-year-old enslaved crewman, and the white officers were spending the night onshore. Smalls and the other enslaved crew had been planning this in secret. They steamed to a second wharf, picked up their wives and children, and headed into the harbor, sixteen people in all. They knew the route and the signals, and they used them. At Fort Sumter, Smalls gave the proper signal, and the Planter was allowed to pass. Once clear, they turned toward the Union blockade and raised a white bedsheet. A Union ship had been getting ready to fire. Instead, the Planter, and the guns it carried, became a Union vessel. Congress voted the crew a share of the ship's value, and Smalls received fifteen hundred dollars. He went on to pilot for the Union, and later served five terms in the U.S. House of Representatives. The Confederacy depended on enslaved sailors to run its harbor. That knowledge is how they escaped.
